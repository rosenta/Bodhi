# Peeling the Onion

"Tell me what you are."

The Guru asked it the way one asks the time. Casually. As if the answer were on a nearby wall.

They were sitting cross-legged on the hut's packed-earth floor. Noon. The thatched roof pressed the heat down on them like a hand. The air smelled of dry grass and old woodsmoke and the faintly sweet breath of fruit going slightly soft in a clay bowl. Outside, the cicadas kept up their one-note argument with the sun.

The disciple straightened. He had been waiting for this question all week.

"I am a man. Forty-one years old. Six feet. Brown eyes. There is a scar on my left knee from a bicycle I fell off when I was nine."

The Guru picked up a mango from the bowl. Turned it slowly between his palms, examining it for bruises.

"If I cut off your arm," he said to the mango, "would you be less yourself."

"No. Not essentially."

"If disease wasted your legs and you could not stand again — would the one answering me now cease to exist."

"No."

"Then you are not the body." The Guru set the mango back. "The body is food. Made of food. Sustained by food. Returning to food. It is the outermost sheath — the *annamaya kosha* — and you wear it the way you wear a shirt. When the shirt tears, you are not torn. Try again. What are you."

The floor was hard under his sitting bones. A bead of sweat started at his hairline and began its slow crawl down his temple. He tracked it without meaning to.

"I am — alive. I breathe. There is a vitality in me. A force that gets up in the morning, that drives me, that —"

"That force. Does it stay constant."

"No. It rises. It falls. When I am sick it weakens. When I sleep —"

"It disappears. And yet you persist through sleep, yes. Something continues. The vital breath — the *pranamaya kosha* — is another sheath. It animates the body the way wind animates a bellows. The wind is not the blacksmith. Try again."

The disciple felt a small prick of irritation, then a larger one. He had spent twenty years assembling an identity sturdy enough to carry through boardrooms and dinner parties and school plays. Now it was coming apart with the patience of someone pulling petals off a flower to see what was underneath.

"My mind, then. My thoughts. My memory. My intelligence. The thing that analyzes, that decides, that —"

"Whose mind."

"What."

"You said *my* mind. Who owns it."

The disciple blinked. *My.* Such a small word. He had never once noticed the distance it implied — a possessor, and a possession. Two things where he had always assumed there was one.

"I — I don't —"

"The mind is the third sheath. It processes. Reacts. Worries. Schemes. Extraordinary machinery. But machinery. You can watch it, can you not. You can see a thought arise and pass. If you *were* the thought, who would be watching."

The heat in the hut seemed to thicken. His throat was dry. He reached for the small clay cup beside him — the Guru's water — and stopped, embarrassed, and set his hand back on his knee.

Each layer the old man removed did not reveal something solid underneath. It revealed another layer. And beneath *that*, the suggestion of an emptiness that made his palms damp.

"There is something deeper," he said, reaching now. His voice had gone thinner. "My intellect. My capacity to discriminate, to know, to understand. The part of me that says *this is true* and *that is false*."

"Closer." For the first time there was warmth in the old man's voice. Closer, the disciple thought, but not close enough. "The *vijnanamaya kosha.* The sheath of discernment. The subtlest instrument you have. And still an instrument. A telescope is not the eye. Who looks through the telescope."

The disciple did not answer. He could feel something slipping beneath him — not the earthen floor but the other floor, the one he had always assumed he was standing on, the floor of his own self-concept. Every answer he produced was taken away. And what was left was not an answer but a vertigo — the sensation of being at the edge of a cliff and being asked, politely, to describe the drop.

"I don't know," he said finally. The words came out raw, half-angry, half-pleading. "You keep asking me what I am and taking away every answer. *What is left.* There is nothing left."

The Guru leaned forward. His eyes were steady. Kind. Absolutely unyielding.

"There is one more sheath. The deepest. The *anandamaya kosha* — the sheath of bliss. You have touched it in deep sleep. In moments of overwhelming beauty. In the instant after laughter, when the mind goes blank. It feels like the end of the search. It feels like home."

"Yes." It came out almost whispered. "Yes, I know that feeling."

"It is not you either."

Something in the disciple's chest collapsed. Not painfully. More like a breath he had not known he was holding, finally released.

"Bliss comes and goes," the Guru said softly. "In deep sleep it is present. In waking it recedes. That which comes and goes cannot be your nature. Your nature is that which is *always present.* And what is always present is not any of these five layers — it is the awareness in which all five appear. The awareness that watches the body ache, the breath rise, the mind spin, the intellect grasp, the bliss arrive and leave. It watches all of them. It is none of them."

The hut had gone very quiet. Even the cicadas had fallen off.

"The mind creates bondage," the Guru said, "and the same mind creates liberation. The wind that pulled the storm clouds together is the very wind that clears the sky. You do not need a new instrument. You need to turn the instrument you have inward."

The disciple sat. Breath. The faint weight of the body. The chatter of the mind, quieter now but still there. The sharp edge of intellect, still doing its work. And beneath all of it, holding all of it, untouched by any of it — a silence so wide it had no edges.

He did not name it. He did not reach for it.

He let it be there.

"What do you feel," the Guru asked.

He opened his eyes. The hut looked the same. The mango sat, slightly softer, in the clay bowl. The heat pressed on.

"Afraid," he said honestly. "Because if I am not any of those things — I have spent forty-one years defending something that does not exist."

The Guru smiled. That same smile from the first morning. Not comfort. Recognition.

"Now," he said, "we can begin."
