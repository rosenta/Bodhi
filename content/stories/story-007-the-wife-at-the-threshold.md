# The Wife at the Threshold

He packed the bag at three in the morning because he did not trust himself to pack it in daylight.

One change of clothes. A toothbrush. The slim book with the cracked spine his grandmother had given him, still unread. His passport. A stack of bills he had already signed. That was all.

He had stood in the closet for a long time, looking at the shirts. The tailored ones. The cashmere. The wool coat that had cost more than his first car. He had understood, with a clarity that did not feel like his, that none of it was coming. Clothes were for a man who knew who he was. He did not, anymore.

He carried the bag downstairs. Set it by the door. Sat at the kitchen table in the dark, and waited for her to come down.

She came down at six, as she always did, in the blue robe that was beginning to fray at the cuffs. She stopped when she saw him. She saw the bag. She saw his face.

She did not say anything for a long time.

Then she made tea.

She moved around the kitchen the way she had moved around it for nineteen years. The kettle. The cups. The small ceramic bowl of cardamom pods. He watched her hands. He had watched her hands at the altar when they married, and at the hospital when their daughter was born, and in a hundred thousand mornings since — and he realized, sitting there, that he had never actually *looked* at them. He had looked past them the way a man looks past a window to get to the view.

She set the tea in front of him. She sat down across the table.

"How long," she said. Not a question. A request for a number.

"I don't know."

"Six months. A year."

"I don't know."

She looked at him. Her eyes were dry. He had braced for tears and the absence of them was somehow worse.

"Will you come back."

The lie was ready on his tongue — *of course, of course, I'll call you every week, this is temporary* — and he swallowed it. She deserved better than that. And, honestly, he did not know.

"I don't know that either."

She nodded, slowly. Picked up her tea. Held it in both hands the way she did when she was cold, though the kitchen was not cold.

"I knew this was coming."

"How."

"Four months ago. The Patels' anniversary dinner. Someone asked you what you did, and you opened your mouth to say *CEO* and nothing came out. I saw it. You covered. You said the title. But I saw the pause." Her voice was very even. "I have seen you lose things in nineteen years. Deals. Friends. Your father. I had never seen you lose your own title in your own mouth before. That was when I knew."

"Why didn't you say anything."

"Because it wasn't mine to say." She set the cup down. "And because I loved you too much to try to stop it."

Something broke in him that he had not known was still whole.

"I'm not leaving you," he said. His voice went rough. "I need you to hear that. I am not leaving you. I am —"

"You are leaving *yourself*. The version you have been wearing." She said it almost kindly. "I know."

He could not speak. He reached across the table and took her hand, and she let him, and they sat like that — two people who had built a life together, holding hands over the small wreckage of the story they had agreed, for nineteen years, to believe.

"You know what I used to wonder," she said eventually. "When we first married. I used to wonder what would happen if you ever actually got quiet. You were so loud in your own head. Always running. Always building. I used to think — *if he ever sits still for one whole day, what will he find.*" She looked at him. "I think we are about to find out."

He laughed — a short, broken sound, half sob.

"I am so afraid. I don't know what I am doing. I don't know what I am looking for. I just know I cannot keep pretending."

"Good. Pretending was what was killing you. I watched it. For years. It was like watching someone slowly drown in a swimming pool on dry land."

He wiped his face with the back of his hand.

"The house is paid. The accounts — everything is in your name too. You don't need to —"

"I don't care about the house."

"The company will —"

"I don't care about the company."

She stood up. Walked around the table. Stood behind him, and rested her forehead against the back of his head, her hands on his shoulders. He closed his eyes.

"Listen to me." Quiet. Right at his ear. "Whatever you are going to find — find it all the way. Do you understand me. Do not come back halfway. Do not come back a man who went to the mountain and brought back a motto. If you are going to do this, do it fully. Break yourself open. Find the thing. And if the thing you find is that you cannot come back here — I will survive that. I want you to know I will survive that. I will grieve and I will survive. What I will *not* survive is watching you shrink back into a life you are already too big for."

He could not answer. He reached up and held her hands where they rested on his shoulders.

"She is going to ask where you are," his wife said.

"I know."

"I am not going to lie to her."

"I don't want you to."

"I am going to tell her that her father went to look for something he could not find here. And that he loved her enough to go look."

"Tell her —" his voice broke — "tell her I am not running away. Tell her I am running toward. Tell her when I come back — *if* I come back — it will be with more of me, not less."

She bent her head against his and held him that way, and he could feel her breath on his neck, and he noticed, small and sharp, that she was the one holding *him* up.

"Sons and others can repay a father's debts," she said softly — she had found the book, sometime in the last four months; she had been reading it; he had not known — "but there is no one other than oneself who can remove one's own bondage. I read that verse. I read it over and over. I wanted it not to be true. I wanted there to be a way I could do this for you. But there isn't. Is there."

"No."

"Then go."

He stood. She stepped back. He looked at her in the blue robe with the fraying cuffs and he memorized her. The small line between her brows. The gray at her temple. The way she was standing — braced, a woman who had known this morning was coming and had dressed for it.

He climbed the stairs and went to his daughter's room and stood in the doorway. She was sleeping with one arm flung over her head, the way she had slept since she was two. He did not go in. He did not trust himself. He watched her breathe, in and out, in and out, and he thought: *among all living beings, human birth is the rarest. And she is rarer still. And I am leaving her to go find out why.*

He came back down. His wife was standing at the door holding his bag.

She handed it to him. He took it.

"Go," she said. And then, very low: "Come back changed, or don't come back."

He walked out onto the porch. The sky was beginning to lighten in the east, the first gray seeping in behind the black trees. He heard the door close behind him. He heard the lock turn — not to keep him out, he understood. To let her be alone with what had just happened to her.

He stood on the porch in the cold. He thought, for the first time in his life, that he knew what the old books meant by renunciation. It was not the renunciation of objects. The house was still his. The money was still his. The shirts hung in the closet, waiting patiently.

What he had renounced was the right to pretend that any of it had been enough.

He walked down the steps. The taxi was not due for eleven minutes. He sat down on the porch step, put his face in his hands, and let himself cry — quietly — for the life he was leaving and for the woman inside who had loved him fiercely enough to open the door and show him the way out.

The taxi came. He got in. He did not look back at the house. He did not need to. He carried the shape of her in the blue robe with him now, the way a man carries water across a desert — carefully, gratefully, knowing it is finite, knowing it is his.

The driver asked where he was going.

He gave the airport.

The driver asked where he was flying.

He gave the country. The city. The road that ran upstream, beside the river, to where the mangoes grow.

The driver whistled softly. "Long way."

"Yes," he said, and looked out at the empty streets of the life he was leaving. "Yes it is."

---

The first light touched the tops of the buildings. The car turned the corner. The house slid behind them.

In the kitchen, his wife sat alone at the table with two cups of cold tea and one unread book, and she did not move for a long time, and she did not weep, and she did not regret. Love that does not know when to let go is not love. It is a kind of holding that becomes a cage.

She had opened the cage. She had watched the bird go.

Somewhere, she believed, he would learn to fly. And whether he came back to her wrist or not — the sky would have him, and that would be enough.

It would have to be.

She picked up the book he had left behind — the slim volume with the cracked spine, the grandmother's gift, forgotten on the counter in his fastidious, terrible haste — and she held it in both hands and read the first verse again, the one about the rarity of human birth, and she thought:

*And rarer still is the one who lets the seeker go.*
