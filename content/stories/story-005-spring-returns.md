# Spring Returns

He did not notice the moment it happened. That was the thing.

He had expected lightning. A crack in the sky. Flooding light. The celestial fanfare every spiritual book seemed to sell on the back cover. He had braced for it the way you brace for a wave — muscles hard, eyes shut, waiting for the impact that would rinse the old self away and leave something luminous and new in its place.

It did not come like that.

It came like spring.

---

On the last morning, the Guru walked him to the river and said nothing for a long time. The water moved. A kingfisher dropped and came up with a flash of silver in its beak and was gone.

"You are ready to go back."

Not a question.

"I don't feel ready."

"Readiness does not feel like readiness. It feels like this. An ordinary morning. An ordinary breath. And the quiet knowledge that the ground beneath you has changed though it looks the same."

The disciple looked at him — the lined face, the thin white hair, the eyes that carried that impossible depth — and felt something swell that he could not name. Gratitude was too small. It was more like recognizing a face you had known before you were born.

"What will be different."

The Guru smiled. "Everything. Nothing. You will see."

---

He came down from the hills on a Tuesday. The same bus. The same fields. The same airport with its fluorescent hum and its scrolling board of destinations. He bought a coffee. Sat in a plastic chair. Watched the crowd move around him.

A businessman wheeling a hard-shell case. A mother adjusting her toddler's sun hat. A young couple arguing in murmurs by the window.

Nothing had changed. And everything had changed.

It was not that the noise was gone. The airport hummed its usual chaos — announcements, the whine of luggage wheels, the small mechanical music of a thousand tiny hurries. But the noise no longer *landed* in him the way it used to. Before, it would have set off the old tightness. The reach for the phone. The impulse to be somewhere other than here. Now the sound moved through him the way wind moves through an open window. Present. Felt. Gone.

He boarded the plane. He slept without effort, for the first time in a decade. He came home.

---

His wife was at the door. She looked at him the way she had looked at him the night he left — carefully, reading small print.

"Are you okay."

"Yes." And for the first time in years it was not a performance.

She tilted her head. Half a smile. "You look — lighter."

He did not try to explain. What could he say. *I sat in a hut and an old man dismantled everything I thought I was, and what was left has no name.* It would sound like madness. Or worse — like a new project, a new vocabulary, another phase.

He just held her. And in the holding he noticed something the teaching had quietly made possible: he was not holding her to feel better. He was not holding her to fill the hollowness. He was holding her because holding her was what was happening, and he was present for it, and that was enough.

---

The marketplace was the test he did not know he was taking.

Saturday morning. He walked into the old quarter — cramped streets, vegetable stalls, fabric shops, the chai seller who shouted his prices as if personally affronted by the existence of competitors. He had always avoided this place. The noise. The crowd. The aggressive intimacy of bodies pressing close in the heat. It had always set off an anxious static in him, a wish to retreat to the controlled silence of an office.

He walked in anyway.

The noise rose around him. Vendors called. A motorbike threaded between a cart and a child. Somewhere a transistor radio played a song he half-remembered from some summer twenty years ago. A small girl tugged at his sleeve, selling jasmine garlands, and he bought one without calculating, pressing a coin into a small brown palm still sticky with something.

And he felt — nothing.

Not numbness. Not detachment. Not the flat ceiling of someone who has shut down. Something else.

The noise was there. The colors were there. The smell of frying oil, marigold, diesel, cardamom tea. All there. But the one who used to be disturbed by all of it — the one who clenched, who judged, who narrated every moment with a running commentary of comparison and complaint — that one had gone quiet. Not dead. Just quiet. Like a lake that had been choppy for forty years and had, without announcement, without drama, gone still.

He stood in the middle of the marketplace and breathed.

*As long as a person identifies with this corpse-like body, they remain impure, subject to suffering. But when they realize their Self as pure, auspicious, and immovable — they are freed from all these.*

The verse surfaced in him like a stone rising through clear water. He was not reciting it. It was simply true — the way the sky is true. He was standing in a body that would age and break, in a marketplace that would one day be dust, and none of it touched the silence at his center. Not because he had escaped the world. Because he had stopped mistaking the world for himself.

A man bumped his shoulder and apologized. The disciple smiled and stepped aside.

He thought of the Guru's words — the ones he had not yet fully understood when he first heard them:

*Great, peaceful souls dwell in this world like the spring season, bringing welfare to all. Having themselves crossed the terrible ocean of worldly existence, they help others cross too, without any selfish motive.*

He was not a great soul. He knew that. He was a man in a marketplace with a jasmine garland in his hand who had spent three weeks in a hut and come back *slightly* different. But he understood the metaphor now, and not as poetry. As instruction.

The Guru had not changed the world around him. The Guru had simply *arrived*, the way spring arrives, and the frozen ground had thawed on its own. Not by argument. Not by force. By presence.

He wondered if he could carry even a fraction of that warmth. Not by teaching — he had nothing to teach. Not by preaching — the words would be all wrong. But by being present. By standing in a marketplace without the static. By listening to his wife without drafting his reply underneath. By looking at his daughter without needing her to fill the hollow place that no longer existed.

He walked home through streets that were the same streets. The sun was the same sun. The body was the same body.

But the inner noise had stopped, and the beautiful thing — the strange and almost embarrassing thing — was that he could not say *when.* There was no trumpet. No flash. Just a slow settling, like sediment drifting to the bottom of a glass, until what remained was clear.

He let himself in through the front door. His daughter was at the kitchen table drawing. She looked up.

"Dad. You look different."

"Do I."

"Yeah. Like you're actually here."

He sat down beside her and watched her draw — a tree, wild and green, birds in every branch. He did not check his phone. He did not think about tomorrow. He sat in his kitchen, in his life, in the only moment that had ever existed, and he was, at last, simply there.

Spring had come.

Not to the world.

To him.

And like all true springs it had arrived without announcement — quietly, persistently, the way warmth returns to frozen ground, the way light fills a room when someone, finally, opens the curtain.
